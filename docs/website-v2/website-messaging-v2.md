## 2. Core problem language

Lead with situations clients recognize rather than consulting categories they may not know they need.

Common symptoms include:

- decisions repeatedly getting stuck;
- too many decisions depending on one person;
- unclear actual authority even when roles are documented;
- agreements failing to turn into execution;
- responsibilities overlapping in practice;
- teams working well separately but poorly across interfaces;
- growth making informal coordination increasingly unreliable;
- recurring tensions being discussed without producing lasting change;
- operational reality failing to reach strategic decision-makers;
- accountability becoming either too weak or overly controlling;
- organizational processes accumulating without solving the underlying bottleneck.

Canonical framing:

> **Your team may not need more process. It may need to understand what is actually preventing work from moving.**

---

## 3. Core thesis

Many coordination failures appear when the way authority, information, commitments, capacity, and responsibility actually move through an organization no longer matches what the organization needs in order to execute and adapt.

The goal is not to install a predetermined organizational model.

The goal is to identify the relevant mechanisms and build the **minimum viable coordination** required for the team to operate reliably while preserving autonomy and adaptability.

## 6. Ways to work together

### A. Systemic Coordination Diagnostic

**Free self-guided tool.**

A private browser-based assessment designed to help a team identify possible coordination mechanisms and useful next steps.

This is not the same as the paid Systemic Coordination Review.

---

### B. Systemic Coordination Review

A deeper professional diagnostic engagement.

Best suited for teams that know something important is not working but are not yet sure where the underlying mechanism sits.

Typical questions:

- Why do decisions keep returning to the same people?
- Why do agreements fail to become action?
- Are role problems actually authority problems?
- Where are information and accountability breaking down?
- Is this primarily structural, relational, capacity-related, or some combination?

Typical process:

1. Context and baseline.
2. Review of relevant organizational evidence.
3. Stakeholder conversations.
4. Reconstruction of concrete coordination failures.
5. Mapping of likely mechanisms.
6. Findings and recommendations.

Potential outputs:

- systemic coordination review;
- authority map;
- coordination bottleneck map;
- evidence from concrete cases;
- relevant consequences;
- prioritized recommendations;
- recommendation to intervene, self-manage or seek another specialist.

Pricing is defined in `commercial-and-privacy-config-v1.md`.

## 7. How I work

### Diagnose before prescribing

Do not assume the team automatically needs:

- more roles;
- more hierarchy;
- more participation;
- more process;
- more autonomy.

First understand what is actually happening.

### Work from concrete situations

Abstract complaints such as “communication is bad” should be traced to actual examples:

- a stuck decision;
- a failed handoff;
- an overridden role;
- an unowned commitment;
- a recurring tension.

### Design the minimum necessary change

Ask:

> **What is the smallest change that could materially improve the system?**

### Experiment in real work

Whenever possible, test new agreements against actual work rather than assuming the design is correct.

### Build self-correction capacity

A successful intervention should make the organization less dependent on the consultant over time.

## 8. Common engagement phases

The exact process changes with context, but the public pattern may be represented as:

### 1. Understand

Establish context, baseline and concrete examples.

### 2. Diagnose

Identify likely mechanisms rather than treating symptoms as causes.

### 3. Design

Define the smallest useful changes.

### 4. Experiment

Test those changes against real work.

### 5. Review

Assess evidence, exceptions and unintended consequences.

### 6. Transfer

Document what should remain and ensure the client can operate it independently.

These phases should be compatible with future machine-readable milestones.

## 9. Verification philosophy

Not every organizational outcome can responsibly be reduced to one KPI.

Progress should nevertheless be inspectable.

### Artifact evidence

Examples:

- diagnostic delivered;
- authority map completed;
- agreement documented;
- experiment log created;
- final review delivered.

### Process evidence

Examples:

- relevant perspectives gathered;
- sponsor reviewed the diagnosis;
- selected changes tested;
- exceptions reviewed.

### Outcome evidence

Context-dependent examples:

- fewer decisions requiring unnecessary escalation;
- shorter decision turnaround;
- fewer reopened decisions;
- fewer unowned commitments;
- fewer recurring handoff failures;
- stronger perceived authority clarity;
- reduced coordination load on founders/managers.

Avoid false precision.
